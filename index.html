Getting started

How to use this map

This map shows where Charlotte’s most serious crashes (those that kill or seriously injure someone) cluster along city streets.

Reading the colors

Streets are shaded by how tightly severe crashes concentrate along them. Gray is low, yellow and orange indicate medium, and red indicates high density.

Explore a corridor

Click any red marker to open a detailed look at one of fourteen selected high-crash corridors, including its crash counts, its rate per mile, and other details about the street. These corridors are selected segments of roads where density appears highest. They are not whole roads, and they are separate from the "Comprehensive corridor data" table described below.

Switch crash types

Use the "Crash mode" controls to switch between all crashes, pedestrian-and-cyclist crashes, pedestrians only, or cyclists only.

Turn the layer on or off

The "Crash density" checkbox hides or shows the colored streets.

Browse major roads

The "Comprehensive corridor data" button opens a sortable table of select major roads with severe crash metrics for each. This lists entire roads selected by fixed criteria (size, length, and speed limit), regardless of whether their density is high or low. This analysis aims to provide an overview of major city streets. This list is not an authoritative list of every busy city road, but aims to be a representative list selected with specific criteria.

For more details on how this analysis was built, see Methodology.

About this map

Charlotte severe-crash density

This map is a visualization of where killed or seriously injured (KSI) crashes concentrate across Charlotte. It provides detail on fourteen select corridors. It is intended as an independent analysis separate from the City's High-Injury Network, which uses different methodology.

There are two ways to examine corridors in this map. The first is via the fourteen featured corridors, which appear as red dots on the map. These corridors are parts of one or several different roads that appeared to have a very high concentration of severe crashes based on the analysis. The second is via the corridor data table under the "Comprehensive corridor data" tab. This is a list of whole roads in Charlotte that met certain criteria, and aims to be a representative list of Charlotte's major arterial roads and their crash data. They were selected via fixed criteria (length, size, and speed limit).

Why Network Kernel Density Estimation (NKDE)?

When cities build crash maps, many simply join crash counts to predetermined road segments (these segments are typically between .25-1 mile long), and then color code those segments based on how many crashes occur inside of these segments. This method is simple, quick, and easy to understand for the viewer. However, it comes with an issue called the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP). The MAUP is a common problem with maps where the visualizations can be altered by changing the boundaries in which they are displayed, without any change to the underlying data. The most famous example of the MAUP is gerrymandering. Politicians can change the visualization of the data (how certain districts vote) by altering the boundaries in which they are displayed (congressional districts) without changing the underlying data (where voters are located). When analyzing crash data, a similar trend emerges. The City of Charlotte's High-Injury Map splits roads into 2500 ft segments, then assigns those segments a color based on a weighted severity index (KSI Score). These splits are arbitrary and done automatically. If you move the boundaries between these splits, you can change which segments appear the most severe without actually changing the crash locations. Since crashes are often influenced by unsafe conditions at a specific point (such as an intersection or a merge point) measuring 2500 foot segments partially influenced by arbitrary line splitting can make it hard to visualize what specific point, if any, is causing accidents.

NKDE does not involve splitting the road network at all. It describes how points (in this case, severe crashes) concentrate along a network (Charlotte's roads) and produces a number describing the density of points at each point in the network. It differs from a conventional planar heatmap in that the density score is constrained by the network. The resolution of this analysis (lixel size) was 150 ft, which is 17 times shorter than the City's. This leads to a mitigation of the effects of the MAUP. Even though boundaries between lixels are randomly assigned, crashes in one lixel affect the score of adjacent lixels because the density in those lixels is also affected. Moving the boundaries would result in insignificant changes to the map. This contrasts to the simple approach, in which segments are not in any way affected by incidents happening in adjacent segments.

Why count severe crashes as opposed to a weighted score?

The City's High-Injury Network applies a weighted score per segment reflecting incidents that happen within it. The score assigns a score of 5 for any fatality, a score of 3 for a pedestrian or cyclist that was seriously injured, and a score of 1 for a motorist that was severely injured. This map counts all severe (KSI) crashes equally regardless of their characteristics. This is for two reasons. First, a per-person weighing can be influenced by how many people were in affected vehicles. This is an external factor, and is not a function of road design. Second, the significant difference in the city's weighing between a fatality and a motorist injury may place too much emphasis on fatalities, while discounting severe injuries. In many cases, the difference between a severe injury and death is not due to road design. This difference can be explained by emergency response time, the victim's age and health, and simply luck. There are too many factors that influence this difference that are not due to road design, and the degree to which road design affects this difference is unknown. Counting all severe crashes equally best isolates the effect on road conditions on crash concentration, and can identify dangerous corridors before they produce fatalities.

Colors

The map includes a color scale that shows the calculated density of KSI crashes along each street. Gray indicates little or no crash density. Yellow and orange indicate a moderate concentration, while red spots indicate a high concentration. The breaks between colors are consistent between mode selection. The corridor data table uses the same palette applied differently. Each table column is broken into quartiles based on the data in the column. The lowest quartile is assigned gray, the highest red, and yellow and orange in the middle.

Choosing Corridors

The map includes fourteen highlighted corridors. They are parts of one or many roads that were selected based on visual appearance of severity, and are not an authoritative claim as to which roads are empirically the most dangerous. They are not exhaustive and are not ranked. The corridors each have a corresponding analysis when clicked. They differ from the corridor data table, which aims to list most arterial roads in Charlotte by setting certain criteria for inclusion.

Corridor Data Table

The "Comprehensive corridor data" tab includes a table listing many corridors and their associated crash data. These are whole roads selected by fixed criteria, not their concentration of crashes. A road is included if a substantial portion is four or more lanes, the modal posted speed is 35mph or higher, and it runs two or more continuous miles within city limits. Interstate highways were excluded. Limited access freeways that do not meet interstate standards (Billy Graham Parkway, Independence Boulevard, and parts of the Brookshire Freeway) were included. 57 corridors met these requirements.

Charlotte's street names are famously convoluted and inconsistent. A straight drive down a road can result in multiple name changes, and directional prefixes are applied inconsistently. Because of this, some separate roads were grouped into the same corridor. This was determined by two criteria. First, the road must be continuous. A road that is directionally continuous with different directional prefixes but the same name was treated as continuous for this purpose. Second, each half was analyzed to determine if each half is long enough to stand on its own as a substantial corridor. If they were both long enough, the roads were kept separate (as is the case with North and South Tryon). If this was not the case, the segments were merged (as is the case for North and South Wendover).

Crashes that occur within 50 feet of a corridor are attributed to that corridor to account for variations in location. This means that crashes near an intersection of two corridors may count for both. Crash columns in the table are shaded by quartile (except for the length of the corridor).

Corridor Statistics

For the 14 highlighted corridors, panels show fatalities, serious injuries, total KSI, and KSI per mile. Because some corridors are short, their per mile rates are very sensitive. KSI per mile should be treated as illustrative and not a precise measure of safety. Corridors that share an intersection may double count crashes at that junction.

Data and Software

Crash points come from the City of Charlotte's Fatal or Serious Injury Crashes dataset, and range from 2020-2024. The street network is the City of Charlotte's street centerline layer. Density was estimated with the spNetwork package in R. Symbology and corridor shapes were done using ArcGIS Pro. The figures found in the introduction card come from the City of Charlotte's FY2025 Vision Zero Annual Report.

This is a set of select major roads in Charlotte with details about the crashes that happen on them. A road is included if a substantial portion is four or more lanes, its typical posted speed limit is 35 mph or higher, and it runs two or more miles within city limits. Interstate highways are excluded. This is not a comprehensive or authoritative list of every major or busy road. There are 57 roads included, which account for 558 of the 839 KSI crashes citywide (about 67%). Note that a crash near an intersection of two corridors may count for both.
