About this map

Charlotte severe-crash density

A network-based view of where killed-or-seriously-injured (KSI) crashes concentrate across Charlotte's streets, with detail on fourteen high-injury corridors. It is intended as an independent, complementary lens on the City's own High-Injury Network — not a replacement for it. The two examine the same problem with different methods, and agreement between them is a useful signal.

Why network kernel density

Joining crash counts to road segments makes results depend on where the segment breaks are drawn — a form of the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. The same dangerous stretch can look moderate when split across several short segments, or be diluted when averaged into one long one, so the apparent pattern partly reflects the segmentation rather than the crashes themselves. A kernel density estimate avoids pre-imposing those units: it describes how severe crashes concentrate as a continuous surface along the network. The estimate is network-constrained — distances follow the street network rather than straight-line proximity — so crashes on a nearby parallel road do not bleed into a corridor the way a conventional heatmap would allow.

Severe crashes are counted, not weighted

The City's High-Injury Network scores crashes with a weighted severity index applied per person killed or seriously injured. This surface instead counts severe (KSI) crashes equally, for two reasons. First, a per-person weighting partly reflects how many people were in the vehicles — an occupancy factor, not a function of road design. Second, whether a severe crash ends in death or serious injury often turns on factors outside the road's control, such as trauma-response time, a victim's age, and chance. Counting severe crashes equally keeps the measure tied to road conditions and treats a corridor as dangerous before it produces a fatality. The City's weighting remains the appropriate tool for prioritizing where to invest; this is a complementary spatial read.

What the colors show

The warm ramp is the estimated density of KSI crashes along each street. Gray streets carry little or no severe-crash density; yellow, orange, and red mark rising concentration.

Choosing the fourteen corridors

The fourteen highlighted corridors are illustrative examples drawn from the areas of most concentrated density — the strongest, most sustained clusters on the surface. They are not exhaustive and not ranked: other corridors also show elevated density. These fourteen were annotated to illustrate the range of conditions where severe crashes concentrate, and each is examined in detail. The density surface itself, not this set, is the complete picture.

Corridor statistics

For each highlighted corridor, the panel shows fatalities, serious injuries, total KSI, and KSI per mile (counts normalized by corridor length). Per-mile rates let short and long corridors be compared on equal footing; because some corridors are short, their per-mile rates are more sensitive to individual crashes and should be read as indicative rather than precise. Corridors that share an intersection may double-count crashes at that junction.

Data & method

Crash records are killed-or-seriously-injured crashes, 2020–2024, from the City of Charlotte. The density surface was produced with the spNetwork package in R. This is an exploratory screening analysis, meant to surface where severe crashes concentrate rather than to serve as an exposure-normalized or causal model; individual corridor rankings should be read as indicative.

The 13% / 80% / 29% figures cited elsewhere are the City of Charlotte's own findings, from its FY25 Vision Zero Annual Report, and describe the City's High-Injury Network — a separate analysis from the density estimate shown here.

Mode layers share one common color scale (the all-mode class breaks), so a given color denotes the same density across every layer. Because pedestrian and cyclist crashes are fewer, those layers read lower on the shared scale by design — this preserves true relative magnitude rather than re-normalizing each layer to itself. The cyclist layer in particular rests on a small number of crashes and should be read as indicative only.
