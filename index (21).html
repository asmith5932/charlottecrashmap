Getting started

How to use this map

This map shows where Charlotte's most serious crashes — those that kill or seriously injure someone — cluster along city streets.

Reading the colors

Streets are shaded by how tightly severe crashes concentrate along them. Gray is low, yellow and orange indicate medium, and red indicates high density.

Explore a corridor

Click any red marker to open a detailed look at one of fourteen selected high-crash corridors, including its crash counts, its rate per mile, and other details about the street. These corridors are selected segments of roads where density appears highest. They are not whole roads, and they are separate from the "Comprehensive corridor data" table described below.

Switch crash types

Use the "Crash mode" controls to switch between all crashes, pedestrian-and-cyclist crashes, pedestrians only, or cyclists only.

Turn the layer on or off

The "Crash density" checkbox hides or shows the colored streets.

Browse major roads

The "Comprehensive corridor data" button opens a sortable table of select major roads with severe crash metrics for each. This lists entire roads selected by fixed criteria (size, length, and speed limit), regardless of whether their density is high or low. This analysis aims to provide an overview of major city streets. This list is not an authoritative list of every busy city road, but aims to be a representative list selected with specific criteria.

For more details on how this analysis was built, see Methodology.

About this map

Charlotte severe-crash density

A network-based view of where killed-or-seriously-injured (KSI) crashes concentrate across Charlotte's streets, with detail on fourteen high-injury corridors. It is intended as an independent, complementary lens on the City's own High-Injury Network — not a replacement for it. The two examine the same problem with different methods, and agreement between them is a useful signal.

Two different things are shown here, and they should not be confused. The fourteen featured corridors are hand-picked parts of roads — specific high-density stretches, chosen by eye. The corridor data table ("Comprehensive corridor data") is a list of whole roads selected by fixed size-and-length criteria, whose density may be high or low. The fourteen are worked examples of dangerous stretches; the table is a broad inventory of major roads. Their crash counts are measured over different lengths and are not directly comparable.

Why network kernel density

Joining crash counts to road segments makes results depend on where the segment breaks are drawn — a form of the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem. The same dangerous stretch can look moderate when split across several short segments, or be diluted when averaged into one long one, so the apparent pattern partly reflects the segmentation rather than the crashes themselves. A kernel density estimate avoids pre-imposing those units: it describes how severe crashes concentrate as a continuous map along the network. The estimate is network-constrained — distances follow the street network rather than straight-line proximity — so crashes on a nearby parallel road do not bleed into a corridor the way a conventional heatmap would allow.

Concretely, the City's High-Injury Network assigns each crash a severity-weighted score and aggregates those scores into roughly 2,500-ft roadway segments, then ranks them. This map instead breaks the network into 150-ft lixels — segments roughly seventeen times shorter — and estimates a smooth density across them. The finer resolution means a short, intensely dangerous stretch is less likely to be averaged into a longer, calmer segment, and the continuous estimate doesn't hinge on where any single segment happens to begin or end.

Severe crashes are counted, not weighted

The City's High-Injury Network scores crashes with a weighted severity index applied per person killed or seriously injured. This map instead counts severe (KSI) crashes equally, for two reasons. First, a per-person weighting partly reflects how many people were in the vehicles — an occupancy factor, not a function of road design. Second, whether a severe crash ends in death or serious injury often turns on factors outside the road's control, such as trauma-response time, a victim's age, and chance. Counting severe crashes equally keeps the measure tied to road conditions and treats a corridor as dangerous before it produces a fatality.

What the colors show

On the map, the color scale shows the estimated density of KSI crashes along each street. Gray streets carry little or no severe-crash density; yellow, orange, and red mark rising concentration, using fixed density thresholds that are identical across all four crash-mode layers so they can be compared directly.

The corridor data table reuses the same four-color palette, but applies it differently: rather than fixed density thresholds, each table column is shaded by quartile — the lowest quarter of corridors gray, the highest quarter red, scaled within that column. So the map's colors mean "how concentrated," while the table's colors mean "how this corridor ranks against the others in that column." Same palette, two different jobs.

Choosing the fourteen corridors

The fourteen highlighted corridors are illustrative examples — hand-picked parts of roads, not whole roads. Each is a specific stretch drawn from the areas of most concentrated density, chosen by eye rather than a measured cutoff. They are not exhaustive and not ranked: other stretches also show elevated density. These fourteen were annotated to illustrate the range of conditions where severe crashes concentrate, and each is examined in detail. They are distinct from the corridor data table below, which lists whole roads by criteria; the density map itself, not either list, is the complete picture.

The corridor data table

Separately from the density map and the fourteen featured stretches, the "Comprehensive corridor data" table reports raw severe-crash counts for a consistent set of Charlotte's major arterials. The crucial difference: these are whole roads selected by fixed criteria — not high-density stretches picked by eye. A road appears here because it meets the test, whether its crash density turns out high or low. A road is included if a substantial portion is four or more lanes, its typical (modal) posted speed is 35 mph or higher, and it runs two or more miles within city limits; Interstate highways are excluded. This was a lanes-speed-length screen applied with manual review, not a strict road-classification query, and the four-lane extent was confirmed by inspection, since brief turn-lane widenings can otherwise register as false four-lane roads. That yields 57 corridors — not an exhaustive list of arterials, just a consistent way to surface the big roads.

Because Charlotte street names are inconsistent — names change along a single road, and directional prefixes are applied unevenly — segments were grouped into corridors by hand, governed by two questions. First, is it the same continuous road? Directional halves of one road (North/South, East/West) are the same road; genuinely different names are kept separate even where the road is physically continuous (otherwise a single route like Tyvola–Fairview–Sardis–Rama–Idlewild would collapse into one corridor), and roads that merely share a name but are physically distinct stay apart. Second, if it is one road, its directional halves are merged — unless each half is long enough to stand alone as a substantial corridor (as with North and South Tryon), in which case they are kept separate.

Each crash is attributed to a corridor if it falls within 50 feet of it, so a crash near an intersection of two corridors may count toward both. Together these 57 corridors capture 558 of the 839 KSI crashes citywide, about 67%. Because the table measures each full arterial across its length, its counts are larger than — and shouldn't be compared directly to — the fourteen featured corridors, which describe shorter, specific high-density segments. Every crash column in the table (total KSI, fatal, serious, pedestrian, cyclist, and KSI per mile) is shaded independently by quartile: within that one column, the lowest quarter of corridors is gray, then yellow, orange, and red for the highest quarter. Each column is shaded against itself, so a red in the cyclist column and a red in the KSI column represent very different absolute counts — the color marks rank within the measure, not an absolute threshold. Length in miles is shown but not shaded.

Corridor statistics

For each highlighted corridor, the panel shows fatalities, serious injuries, total KSI, and KSI per mile (counts normalized by corridor length). Per-mile rates let short and long corridors be compared on equal footing; because some corridors are short, their per-mile rates are more sensitive to individual crashes and should be read as indicative rather than precise. Corridors that share an intersection may double-count crashes at that junction.

Data & method

Crash records are killed-or-seriously-injured crashes, 2020–2024, from the City of Charlotte's Fatal or Serious Injury Crashes open dataset. The street network is the City of Charlotte / Mecklenburg County street centerline layer. The density map was produced with the spNetwork package in R. This is an exploratory screening analysis, meant to surface where severe crashes concentrate rather than to serve as an exposure-normalized or causal model; individual corridor rankings should be read as indicative.

The 13% / 80% / 29% figures cited elsewhere are the City of Charlotte's own findings, from its FY25 Vision Zero Annual Report, and describe the City's High-Injury Network — a separate analysis from the density estimate shown here.
